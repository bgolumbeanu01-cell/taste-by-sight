The science behind the plates

You taste with your eyes.

People link colours, shapes and textures to tastes and smells in consistent ways. Researchers call these crossmodal correspondences. Every plate in this test is built from cues that have held up in published studies, several of them run on specialty coffee.

  1. 01

    Colour → taste

    452 people in China, India, Malaysia and the USA matched pink to sweet, green to sour, black to bitter and white to salty. Pink–sweet and black–bitter held in every country.

    Wan et al., 2014 ↗
  2. 02

    Shape → taste

    Rounded shapes like clouds read as sweet. Angular shapes, crosses and squares read as bitter or sour.

    Wan et al., 2014 ↗
  3. 03

    Shape → smell

    Lemon and pepper smells were matched to angular shapes, raspberry and vanilla to round ones. More intense smells pulled toward angular forms.

    Hanson-Vaux, Crisinel & Spence, 2013 ↗
  4. 04

    Cup colour → coffee

    Across 457 people, the colour of the cup changed how sweet and acidic a Brazilian and a Kenyan specialty coffee were expected and judged to be. When cup colour and coffee clashed, people liked the coffee less.

    Carvalho & Spence, 2019 ↗
  5. 05

    Cup texture → coffee

    The same coffee tasted sweeter from a smooth cup, and more acidic with a drier aftertaste from a rough one. Certified Q-graders were fooled as well as amateurs.

    Carvalho, Moksunova & Spence, 2020 ↗
  6. 06

    Mug colour → intensity

    A latte drunk from a white mug was rated more intense and less sweet than the same latte from a clear or blue one.

    Van Doorn, Wuillemin & Spence, 2014 ↗
  7. 07

    Lightness → intensity

    People pick lighter colours for weaker smells and deeper, more saturated colours for stronger ones.

    Kemp & Gilbert, 1997, in Spence, 2020 ↗
  8. 08

    Texture on its own is weak

    Pictures of textures alone produce only moderate links, such as fluffy with sweet. That is why every plate here stacks colour, shape and texture.

    Barbosa Escobar et al., 2022 ↗

How your result is calculated

Each of the 16 plates carries a score on five dimensions (sweet, bright, floral, intense and cocoa/body) based on its colour, shape and texture cues. A right swipe adds the plate's scores, and a left swipe takes away half. Plates appear in a different random order for everyone to avoid order effects. Totals are normalised per dimension, and you get the nearest of four flavour profiles by cosine similarity.

One caution: people also match things they simply like to round shapes, so part of every result is just liking (Velasco et al.). The research shows that images change expectation and perception, but it hasn't shown that they predict preference. We'll test that in November, blind.
